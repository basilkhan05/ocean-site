Ocean A phone you talk to, so you can look up.

This is the entire home screen.

There are no apps to find and no badges to clear. When the phone wakes, the agent is what logs in, and everything else, from the radios to the pixels, is a tool it uses for you.

For a generation, phones were grids of destinations. You carried context from one app to the next and did all the tapping yourself. Ocean does that part now. You say what you want in a sentence, and it happens. When seeing is faster than hearing, it draws a card, a diff, a map, or a page, and when you're done, it's gone.

The device

One object, designed to be looked at less.

Ocean is one device, with the hardware and the operating system designed together. On a good day you barely look at it, and that calm comes from the whole object: what it can hear, what it chooses to show, and what it keeps to itself.

Listening

It hears you across a loud street.

An array of far-field microphones forms a beam toward your voice and lets the traffic fall away, so you can speak at the volume you'd use with a friend beside you. Your voice is isolated on the device itself, and what it hears goes no further unless you ask.

Display

A beautiful screen that is usually dark.

When it lights, the display is bright and exact, with blacks that vanish into the glass. Most of the day it shows nothing at all. Ocean spends pixels only when seeing beats hearing, then lets the glass go dark again.

Silicon

Fast, private work happens inside.

Listening and triage run on silicon designed for them, and so does the audit log, so quick things feel instant and personal things stay on the phone. For harder thinking, Ocean calls a frontier model in the cloud as a coprocessor and sends only what the task requires.

There is one Ocean device, and the software was written for it alone.

Everything is a commit.

Every action Ocean takes is written to an append-only log on your device, each entry chained to the one before it by its hash. Nothing is edited after the fact, and every line is yours to read.

Words that arrive from outside, in a message, a web page, or a caller's voice, are read as data. Only you give instructions, and anything sensitive waits for your yes.

The mission

1,000,000 people 1,000,000,000 hours a year, given back

Roughly half of a knowledge worker's week goes to coordination: triage, navigation, status checks, and carrying context from one tool to another. That is about twenty hours a week, or a thousand hours a year, for each person.

Ocean exists to give those hours back to a million people. Together that is more than 114,000 years of human time, returned every year.
